Step 13 of 18
Shower pan: set it, drain it, line the stall

Passenger side, rear end: after the walls and W3 are framed and the subfloor is down, before the toilet and the nook.

You bought the pan (36 x 24 in), so this page is about setting it, not building it: bed it, drain it through the floor at the rear-door end, test it, then line the plywood of the stall wall and W3 with a thin waterproof FRP panel and seal every joint with flexible sealant. The liner is FRP paneling, as you chose. Two things are still guesses until you measure: how far out from the wall the drain hole is (5 in from the rear end is yours; 12 out, the middle, is mine) and how tall the pan's lip is against the wall. Change them in the plan's sizes and the drawings and checks follow.

Steps, in order

  1. Measure the pan before anything is cut. Write down its length and depth (36 x 24), the height of its curb and of its lip against the wall, the size of the drain hole and how far it is from the rear end and from the wall (you said the middle of the rear-door end, 5 in from the rear end; the plan has it at 5 in along and 12 out, the middle of the depth, which is my guess). Put the real numbers into the plan's sizes (Shower drain hole, Liner), so the drawings and checks follow.
  2. Look under the floor where the hole goes. Cross-members, wires, brake or fuel lines and the grey tank's place all matter before you drill. The hole at the middle of the rear-door end is near the rear bumper area: check it clears the frame.
  3. Check and prepare the floor. The subfloor under the pan must be flat to 1/8 in over the 36 x 24 in (a 4 ft level or a straightedge). Fill any dip with a flexible leveling filler or shim it; the pan's floor is thin and a fiberglass or acrylic pan must be supported all over, or it cracks where you stand.
  4. Drill the drain hole. Dry-fit the pan, mark the center of its drain hole on the floor, take the pan out, drill a pilot hole through the subfloor and the steel, then cut with a hole saw 1/4 in bigger than the drain body. Deburr the steel edge, treat it with rust converter and epoxy primer, and seal the cut edge of the subfloor with epoxy. Vacuum all the chips out of the van.
  5. Bed the pan. Run zig-zag beads of the flexible adhesive over the subfloor about every 3 in (keep it 2 in back from the hole), bed the drain flange in neutral silicone on the pan, set the pan down against the plywood wall and a hair short of W3, and press it. Check it is level side to side and falls to the drain. Weigh it down (jugs of water, 100 lb) for the adhesive's open time and wipe the squeeze-out.
  6. Connect the drain from below: gasket and lock nut on the body, then the tailpiece, the P-trap and the hose to the grey tank, with a vent on the tank. Keep the hose falling all the way to the tank.
  7. Seal the pan to the wall. A bead of flexible sealant where the pan lip meets the plywood, and the plywood edge below the liner sealed with epoxy (drawing B).
  8. Fill test. Plug the drain, fill the pan to just under the curb, mark the water line and leave it for a day. Check the line has not dropped and look under the floor for any drip. Then open the drain, check it flows freely and leaks nowhere at the nut or the trap.
  9. Prepare the plywood before the liner goes on. (1) Two coats of thin epoxy on every cut edge and on the bare plywood of the nook and of the valve and head cut-outs: water gets in from the edges. (2) Make the face flat. The screws that hold the plywood have finish washers, and a washer would show through a 0.09 in panel as a bump and stop it sticking: in the stall, countersink each screw (or refit it with a flat head) and fill the dimple with epoxy filler, then sand it flush; sand off raised grain, fill any gouge, and check with a straightedge that nothing stands more than about 1/32 in proud, because FRP shows every bump. (3) Vacuum the dust and wipe the face with a damp cloth: it must be clean, dry and free of oil where the adhesive goes (scuff-sand any epoxy that reached the face).
  10. Cut the liner (drawing E): the wall panel (35 1/4 x 69 15/16, with the nook cut out), W3's face (24 x 69 15/16) and the four nook sides, each 1/8 in short at a free edge and 1/4 in above the pan. Cut the head and valve holes (drawing H) the same way.
  11. Cut the nook opening (drawing G), flat on the bench before the panel goes on the wall. (1) Lay the panel face up on a sheet of foam board or a sacrificial plywood sheet, so the cut is supported all round. (2) Measure from the panel's own trimmed edges, not from the wall: the opening starts 12 3/8 in from the rear edge (the nook is 12 1/2 in from the back mark, less the 1/8 in the panel is short) and 20 in down from the top edge (the nook top is at 54, the panel top at 74), and it is 17 1/4 wide by 16 tall. Mark it in pencil and check that both diagonals agree to 1/16 in. (3) Put painter's tape over the line (less chipping) and on the base plate of the saw (no scratches). (4) Drill a 1/2 in hole in each corner, centered 1/4 in in from both lines, so each corner becomes a 1/4 in radius that does not crack. (5) Cut from hole to hole with a jigsaw and a fine-tooth blade (about 10 to 12 teeth to the inch, or a reverse-tooth blade, which leaves the face cleaner), orbital action off, slow speed, in the order of the red numbers; hold the cut-out up for the last side so it cannot drop and tear the corner. (6) Take the saw marks off the edges with a fine file or 120-grit paper, because a rough edge starts a crack. (7) Save the cut-out: it is the nook back.
  12. Cut the head and valve holes (drawing H), flat on the bench before gluing. (1) Mark them from the panel's own trimmed edges: the head hole's center is 8 7/8 in from the rear edge and 4 in down from the top edge (the head is 9 in along and 70 up), and the valve hole's is 32 7/8 in from the rear edge and 30 in down (the valve is 33 along and 44 up). (2) Check them against the real plumbing before you cut: the stubs move a little when they are roughed in, so the pipe's true position beats the plan. Dry-fit the panel on its spacers and compare, or tape a cardboard template to the wall, mark the centers through it and carry them to the panel. (3) Size each hole from your own parts, not from mine: 1/4 in bigger than the part that goes through (a 1/2 in shower arm is 7/8 in across, so 1 1/8 in; a single-handle valve cartridge is about 2 in, so 2 1/4 in), and small enough that the flange or trim plate that hides it overlaps the hole by at least 1/2 in all round (an arm flange is about 2 1/2 in; measure your valve plate). (4) Put painter's tape over the mark, lay the panel face up on foam board or scrap plywood, and drill from the face with a bi-metal or carbide hole saw at slow speed and light pressure, holding the saw square; stop and let it cool if it bites. Never use a twist bit, which cracks the panel. (5) Smooth the edge of each hole with a file. (6) After gluing, put a ring of flexible sealant on the back of each flange or plate, not in the gap around the pipe, so nothing can run behind it; the trim screws into its own body, never into the panel alone.
  13. Glue the wall panel with the panel maker's adhesive (read its can for the trowel, the open time and the temperature). (1) Let the panels sit in the van for at least 24 hours first, so they match its temperature and humidity, and glue at room temperature (about 60 F or more, or what the can says). (2) Dry-fit it: rest the bottom edge on 1/4 in spacers at the pan and keep 1/8 in off W3, the rear edge and the ceiling. (3) Spread the adhesive on the plywood, never on the panel, with the notched trowel or roller the can names, only as far as you can cover within the open time, and keep it about 1 in back from the edges so none squeezes out at the gaps. (4) Set the panel on its spacers, tip the top in, press it flat from the middle outward and roll the whole face with a hand roller, edges last. (5) Hold the top with a few strips of masking tape for the first hour, and leave it alone for the cure time on the can. No screws go through the face: they leak and stop the panel from moving, and the mouldings and the sealant hold the edges. (6) Then the nook (back, then sides), then W3's face, then the inside corner moulding with sealant behind each flange (drawing F).
  14. Seal and trim. Fill the 1/4 in gap at the pan with flexible sealant and tool it; seal the rear edge under its edge trim, the top under the trim strip, every nook corner, and behind the head and valve flanges; wipe the excess off straight away. Let it cure a day.
  15. Seal around the valve and the head (drawing I), after the panel is glued and the valve and the shower arm are in, with the trim plates off. (1) Wipe the panel around each hole and the back of each plate with isopropyl alcohol and let it dry. (2) Dry-fit the plate: it must cover its hole by at least 1/2 in all round. (3) Run a continuous 1/8 in bead of neutral-cure silicone or MS polymer on the back of the plate, in a closed ring about 1/4 in in from its edge, with no gaps (overlap the ends). (4) Press the plate on and tighten its own screws, which go into the valve fitting and never into the panel alone, until the bead squeezes out evenly all round; do not over-tighten, which cracks the FRP or the plate. (5) Stick painter's tape on the panel 1/4 in outside the plate's edge, run a thin bead in the gap between the tape and the plate, smooth it in one pass with a wet finger or a tool, and pull the tape off before it skins. (6) Leave the cartridge and the handle alone: the valve's own seals look after them. (7) Wait a day, then run the shower for ten minutes and look for wet behind (from under the floor or through the valve access); touch up any pinhole. Check these beads once a year and redo any that crack or go black.
  16. Finish and test. Fit the head and valve, hang the curtain and the nook cord, then run the shower for ten minutes with the curtain closed and check under the floor and along the bottom of W3 for any wet.
