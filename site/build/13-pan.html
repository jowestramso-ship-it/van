Step 13 of 18
Shower pan: set it, drain it, line the stall

Passenger side, rear end: after the walls are framed and the subfloor is down, before the toilet, the curtains and the nook.

You bought the pan (36 x 24 in), so this page is about setting it, not building it: bed it, drain it through the floor at the rear-door end, test it, then line the back wall of the stall (the plywood on the passenger wall, and the shower nook) with ONE solid 4 x 8 ft sheet of PVC paneling, with no joints, and seal every edge with flexible sealant. The shower valve is mounted on a small hinged door in the sheet, held shut by four stainless screws, so that its back, its two elbows and the lines can be reached for a leak without taking the wall apart. Only the back wall is lined: the other three sides of the stall are curtains, so there is no end wall W3 and no corner. The lining is PVC paneling, as you chose. Two things are still guesses until you measure: how far out from the wall the drain hole is (5 in from the rear end is yours; 12 out, the middle, is mine) and how tall the pan's lip is against the wall; and the thickness of the sheet (5/16 in is a placeholder: a 4 x 8 sheet is often thinner). Change them in the plan's sizes and the drawings and checks follow.

Steps, in order

  1. Measure the pan before anything is cut. Write down its length and depth (36 x 24), the height of its curb and of its lip against the wall, the size of the drain hole and how far it is from the rear end and from the wall (you said the middle of the rear-door end, 5 in from the rear end; the plan has it at 5 in along and 12 out, the middle of the depth, which is my guess), and the thickness of the PVC sheet (5/16 in is a placeholder: a 4 x 8 sheet is often 1/8 in or less). Put the real numbers into the plan's sizes (Shower drain hole, Liner), so the drawings and checks follow.
  2. Look under the floor where the hole goes. Cross-members, wires, brake or fuel lines and the grey tank's place all matter before you drill. The hole at the middle of the rear-door end is near the rear bumper area: check it clears the frame.
  3. Check and prepare the floor. The subfloor under the pan must be flat to 1/8 in over the 36 x 24 in (a 4 ft level or a straightedge). Fill any dip with a flexible leveling filler or shim it; the pan's floor is thin and a fiberglass or acrylic pan must be supported all over, or it cracks where you stand.
  4. Drill the drain hole. Dry-fit the pan, mark the center of its drain hole on the floor, take the pan out, drill a pilot hole through the subfloor and the steel, then cut with a hole saw 1/4 in bigger than the drain body. Deburr the steel edge, treat it with rust converter and epoxy primer, and seal the cut edge of the subfloor with epoxy. Vacuum all the chips out of the van.
  5. Bed the pan. Run zig-zag beads of the flexible adhesive over the subfloor about every 3 in (keep it 2 in back from the hole), bed the drain flange in neutral silicone on the pan, set the pan down against the plywood wall with its rear end at the back mark (the cab end is just a curtain, so nothing stops it there: measure 36 in from the back mark), and press it. Check it is level side to side and falls to the drain. Weigh it down (jugs of water, 100 lb) for the adhesive's open time and wipe the squeeze-out.
  6. Connect the drain from below: gasket and lock nut on the body, then the tailpiece, the P-trap and the hose to the grey tank, with a vent on the tank. Keep the hose falling all the way to the tank.
  7. Seal the pan to the wall. A bead of flexible sealant where the pan lip meets the plywood, and the plywood edge below the sheet sealed with epoxy (drawing B).
  8. Fill test. Plug the drain, fill the pan to just under the curb, mark the water line and leave it for a day. Check the line has not dropped and look under the floor for any drip. Then open the drain, check it flows freely and leaks nowhere at the nut or the trap.
  9. Fit the frame behind the valve door, and cut the opening in the plywood. (1) Two small pieces of 1x2 furring (VD1 and VD2 in the cut list) go behind the plywood, in the same layer as the furring rails: the cab-side jamb, 9 3/4 in long, upright between S4 and S5 at 33 1/2 to 35 in along (S4's top edge is at 32 up and S5's bottom edge at 41 3/4), and a 6 in piece in line under the nook frame V2, at 24 to 25 1/2 in along and 32 to 38 up, as the rear jamb. Fit them with the furring, before the plywood goes on, each with two 2 in screws driven at an angle into the rail (if the plywood is already on this part of the wall, take its piece off, fit the jambs and put it back). The opening then has wood all round it: S4 below, S5 above, V2 and its extension at the rear, the jamb at the cab side. (2) Mark the opening on the plywood, 8 x 9 3/4 in at 25 1/2 to 33 1/2 in along and 32 to 41 3/4 in up, and cut it out with a jigsaw (drawing G). (3) Two coats of thin epoxy on its four cut edges. The valve door is shown in drawings D and F.
  10. Prepare the plywood before the sheet goes on. (1) Two coats of thin epoxy on every cut edge and on the bare plywood of the nook and of the head's hole: water gets in from the edges. (2) Make the face flat. The screws that hold the plywood have finish washers, and a washer would show through a thin sheet as a bump and stop it sticking: in the stall, countersink each screw (or refit it with a flat head) and fill the dimple with epoxy filler, then sand it flush; sand off raised grain, fill any gouge, and check with a straightedge that nothing stands more than about 1/32 in proud (a solid sheet follows a bump and shows it). (3) Vacuum the dust and wipe the face with a damp cloth: it must be clean, dry and free of oil where the adhesive goes (scuff-sand any epoxy that reached the face). (4) Mark the wall piece's outline on the plywood with a level or a plumb line from the back mark: 1/8 in in from the rear end and from the cab end, 4 1/16 in up (1/4 in over the pan edge) and 74 in up.
  11. Cut the wall piece out of the sheet (drawing E). (1) Let the sheet sit in the van for at least 24 hours first, so it matches its temperature (PVC moves with heat, about 1/16 in over 8 ft for each 15 F). (2) Lay it face up on foam board and cut the wall piece, 35 3/4 x 69 15/16 in, square, from one corner: it stops 1/4 in above the pan edge and 1/8 in under the trim strip at the ceiling, and 1/8 in short of the end trim at each side. (3) Use a straightedge and a jigsaw with a fine blade, or score a thin sheet with a utility knife several times and snap it. (4) Dry-fit it on the wall, and mark which end is the top.
  12. Cut the nook and the valve opening out of the wall piece (drawing G), flat on the bench before gluing. (1) Measure from the piece's own rear edge and top end, not from the wall: the nook starts 11 7/8 in from the rear edge and 20 in down from the top end, and is 12 in wide and 16 in tall; the valve opening starts 25 3/8 in from the rear edge and 32 1/4 in down, and is 8 in wide and 9 3/4 in tall. (2) Put painter's tape over the lines and on the saw's base plate. (3) The sheet is solid: drill a 3/8 in hole at two opposite corners of each rectangle, inside the waste, and cut from hole to hole with a jigsaw and a fine blade, slowly. (4) Save the nook's cut-out: it is the back of the nook. The opening's cut-out is waste. (5) File the cut edges smooth, round the inside corners a little (about 1/8 in), and seal the cut edges that face the nook with a dab of sealant.
  13. Make the valve door (drawing H). (1) Cut the door board to 9 x 10 3/4 in; if it is plywood, seal every face and edge in epoxy and paint it. (2) Mark the valve hole on it, 4 in from the rear edge and 6 1/4 in down from the top edge (the valve is 29 in along and 36 up), and check it against the real valve before you cut: the stubs move a little when they are roughed in, so the part's true position beats the plan. (3) Size the hole from your own part, not from mine: 1/4 in bigger than the part that goes through (a single-handle valve cartridge is about 2 in, so 2 1/4 in), and small enough that the valve's plate overlaps it by at least 1/2 in all round. Check also that the valve's threaded body is long enough to pass through the 1/2 in board and take its nut or plate. (4) Cut it with a hole saw, slowly and face up on foam board, so the board does not melt or crack; never a twist drill. (5) Fix the valve on the door with its own plate and screws, and connect the two swivel elbows to its inlets on the bench. The head's hole in the sheet is made the same way, 28 7/8 in from the wall piece's rear edge and 4 in down from its top end.
  14. Glue the sheet on. (1) Run the flexible adhesive on the plywood in ribbons about 4 in apart over the whole face of the wall piece, never within 1 in of an edge or of the nook and the opening, and only as much as you can cover in the open time on the cartridge (cool or warm, as it says). (2) Set the sheet on 1/4 in spacers on the pan, its rear edge 1/8 in from the end trim line, plumb with a level, and press it flat from the middle outward, then roll the face firmly; the nook and the opening must line up with their holes in the plywood (and with the nook frames V1 and V2). (3) Hold the edges with strips of masking tape for the first hour, keep the van within the temperatures the cartridge allows while it cures, and wipe any squeeze-out at once. (4) Leave the spacers in until the adhesive has set.
  15. Line the nook. Glue the cut-out to the plywood back of the nook as its back, then its floor, its ceiling and its two sides from the offcuts (the floor and the ceiling are 12 x 4 1/2 in, the sides 16 x 4 1/2 in), each in full ribbons of adhesive and each butted to the back, with a bead of sealant in every inside corner. The front edges are left open, with no lip: a bungee cord across the front keeps things in. Use a 4 in roll: the nook is about 4 1/4 in clear inside the lining.
  16. Hang the door. (1) Stick the foam gasket all round the opening on the front of the sheet, 1/4 in outside the cut edge, so that the door's 1/2 in overlap lies on it. (2) Screw the 6 in hinge to the door's back edge on the cab side (short screws, drilled first) and, with the door held in place on the gasket, screw its wall leaf through the sheet and the plywood into the cab-side jamb (long screws, drilled first). (3) Drill the four corner pilot holes through the door, the gasket, the sheet and the plywood, 1/4 in in from each corner of the door, and screw the four #8 x 2 in stainless screws in until the door is snug on the gasket, not crushed. (4) Check the door opens a full 90 degrees without catching the toilet, the curtain or the head arm, and shuts flat with all four screws in.
  17. Seal and trim. Fit the J-shaped end trim over the sheet edges at the rear end and at the cab end, glued to the plywood, with a bead of sealant in the 1/8 in gap behind each (drawing F); fit the top trim strip under the ceiling; fill the 1/4 in gap at the pan with flexible sealant and tool it; seal every nook corner and behind the head flange; seal the plywood and sheet edges at the opening where the gasket does not cover them; wipe the excess off straight away. Let it cure a day.
  18. Seal around the valve and the head (drawing I), after the sheet is glued and the valve and the shower arm are in, with the trim plates off. (1) Wipe the sheet around the head hole, the door around the valve hole and the back of each plate with isopropyl alcohol and let it dry. (2) Dry-fit the plate: it must cover its hole by at least 1/2 in all round. (3) Run a continuous 1/8 in bead of neutral-cure silicone or MS polymer on the back of the plate, in a closed ring about 1/4 in in from its edge, with no gaps (overlap the ends). (4) Press the plate on and tighten its own screws, which go into the valve fitting and never into the sheet or the door alone, until the bead squeezes out evenly all round; do not over-tighten, which cracks the plate. (5) Stick painter's tape on the surface 1/4 in outside the plate's edge, run a thin bead in the gap between the tape and the plate, smooth it in one pass with a wet finger or a tool, and pull the tape off before it skins. (6) Leave the cartridge and the handle alone: the valve's own seals look after them. (7) Wait a day, then run the shower for ten minutes and look for wet behind: back out the four screws, swing the door open and look at the back of the valve and the two elbows. Check these beads once a year and redo any that crack or go black.
  19. Connect the water and test it with the door open. Push the two PEX lines onto the elbows with the 8 to 12 in of spare line behind the door, close the two shutoffs in the sink cabinet and open them slowly, run the shower with the door OPEN and look at every joint with a dry paper towel; then close the door onto its gasket and screw it down. If a joint ever weeps, the shutoffs and the door are all you need.
  20. Finish and test. Hang the curtains from the ceiling rail and the nook cord (the Wet bath page), then run the shower for ten minutes with the curtains closed and check under the floor and along the bottom of the back wall, and where the cab-end curtain hangs by the fridge, for any wet.
