Step 13 of 18
Shower pan: set it, drain it, line the stall

Passenger side, rear end: after the walls are framed and the subfloor is down, before the toilet, the curtains and the nook.

You bought the pan (36 x 24 in), so this page is about setting it, not building it: bed it, drain it through the floor at the rear-door end, test it, then line the back wall of the stall (the plywood on the passenger wall, and the shower nook) with PVC paneling and seal every joint with flexible sealant. Only the back wall is lined: the other three sides of the stall are curtains, so there is no end wall W3 and no corner. The lining is PVC paneling, as you chose. Two things are still guesses until you measure: how far out from the wall the drain hole is (5 in from the rear end is yours; 12 out, the middle, is mine) and how tall the pan's lip is against the wall; and the thickness of the planks (5/16 in is a placeholder). Change them in the plan's sizes and the drawings and checks follow.

Steps, in order

  1. Measure the pan before anything is cut. Write down its length and depth (36 x 24), the height of its curb and of its lip against the wall, the size of the drain hole and how far it is from the rear end and from the wall (you said the middle of the rear-door end, 5 in from the rear end; the plan has it at 5 in along and 12 out, the middle of the depth, which is my guess), and the thickness and the covering width of the PVC planks (5/16 in and 9 3/4 in are placeholders). Put the real numbers into the plan's sizes (Shower drain hole, Liner), so the drawings and checks follow.
  2. Look under the floor where the hole goes. Cross-members, wires, brake or fuel lines and the grey tank's place all matter before you drill. The hole at the middle of the rear-door end is near the rear bumper area: check it clears the frame.
  3. Check and prepare the floor. The subfloor under the pan must be flat to 1/8 in over the 36 x 24 in (a 4 ft level or a straightedge). Fill any dip with a flexible leveling filler or shim it; the pan's floor is thin and a fiberglass or acrylic pan must be supported all over, or it cracks where you stand.
  4. Drill the drain hole. Dry-fit the pan, mark the center of its drain hole on the floor, take the pan out, drill a pilot hole through the subfloor and the steel, then cut with a hole saw 1/4 in bigger than the drain body. Deburr the steel edge, treat it with rust converter and epoxy primer, and seal the cut edge of the subfloor with epoxy. Vacuum all the chips out of the van.
  5. Bed the pan. Run zig-zag beads of the flexible adhesive over the subfloor about every 3 in (keep it 2 in back from the hole), bed the drain flange in neutral silicone on the pan, set the pan down against the plywood wall with its rear end at the back mark (the cab end is just a curtain, so nothing stops it there: measure 36 in from the back mark), and press it. Check it is level side to side and falls to the drain. Weigh it down (jugs of water, 100 lb) for the adhesive's open time and wipe the squeeze-out.
  6. Connect the drain from below: gasket and lock nut on the body, then the tailpiece, the P-trap and the hose to the grey tank, with a vent on the tank. Keep the hose falling all the way to the tank.
  7. Seal the pan to the wall. A bead of flexible sealant where the pan lip meets the plywood, and the plywood edge below the planks sealed with epoxy (drawing B).
  8. Fill test. Plug the drain, fill the pan to just under the curb, mark the water line and leave it for a day. Check the line has not dropped and look under the floor for any drip. Then open the drain, check it flows freely and leaks nowhere at the nut or the trap.
  9. Prepare the plywood before the planks go on. (1) Two coats of thin epoxy on every cut edge and on the bare plywood of the nook and of the valve and head cut-outs: water gets in from the edges. (2) Make the face flat. The screws that hold the plywood have finish washers, and a washer would show through a 5/16 in plank as a bump and stop it sticking: in the stall, countersink each screw (or refit it with a flat head) and fill the dimple with epoxy filler, then sand it flush; sand off raised grain, fill any gouge, and check with a straightedge that nothing stands more than about 1/16 in proud (the hollow planks bridge a small dip, never a bump). (3) Vacuum the dust and wipe the face with a damp cloth: it must be clean, dry and free of oil where the adhesive goes (scuff-sand any epoxy that reached the face). (4) Mark the joints with a level or a plumb line from the back mark: at 9 3/8, 19 1/8 and 28 7/8 in, the first at the nook's rear edge so that the nook's edges fall on joints; if your planks cover a different width, redo the sum from the nook's rear edge (the plan's size "PVC plank width" does it).
  10. Cut the planks (drawing E). (1) Let the planks sit in the van for at least 24 hours first, so they match its temperature (PVC moves with heat, about 1/16 in over 8 ft for each 15 F). (2) Cut each to 69 15/16 in: it stops 1/4 in above the pan edge and 1/8 in under the trim strip at the ceiling. (3) Rip plank 1 down to 9 1/4 in and plank 4 to 7 in (so each stops 1/8 in short of its end trim), on the edge that is not the tongue. (4) Use a fine-tooth saw (24 teeth per inch) or a jigsaw with a fine blade, a plank at a time, supported all along; a thin plank can be scored with a utility knife and snapped. (5) Dry-fit all four on the wall, tongues toward the cab, and number them 1 to 4 on the back.
  11. Cut the nook out of planks 2 and 3 (drawing G), flat on the bench before gluing. (1) Lay the two planks butted together, face up, on foam board or a sacrificial sheet, so the cut is supported all round. (2) Measure from the planks' own top ends, not from the wall: the nook starts 20 in down from the top (the nook top is at 54, the planks' top at 74) and is 16 in tall; plank 2 is cut through at both lines (it is all inside the nook), and plank 3 is cut through at both lines and then along its length 7 1/2 in from its rear edge (the nook ends at 26 5/8 in, and plank 3 starts at 19 1/8). (3) Put painter's tape over the lines and on the saw's base plate. (4) Make cuts 1 and 2 across both planks with a fine-tooth saw and a square, then cut 3 with a jigsaw and a fine blade, starting it in the kerf of cut 1. (5) Save the two cut-outs: they are the back of the nook, and their tongue and groove still fit each other. (6) File the cut edges smooth, round the inside corners a little, and seal the open flutes of every cut end that faces the nook with a dab of sealant.
  12. Cut the head and valve holes (drawing H), flat on the bench before gluing. (1) Mark each from its own plank: the head hole's center is 6 7/8 in from plank 1's rear edge (the head is 7 in along) and 4 in down from its top end (the head is 70 up); the valve hole's center is 1 5/8 in from plank 2's rear edge (the valve is 11 in along, the joint is at 9 3/8) and 38 in down from its top end (the valve is 36 up). The valve hole then clears the joint by 1/2 in. (2) Check them against the real plumbing before you cut: the stubs move a little when they are roughed in, so the pipe's true position beats the plan. Dry-fit the plank on its spacers and compare, or tape a cardboard template to the wall, mark the centers through it and carry them to the plank. (3) Size each hole from your own parts, not from mine: 1/4 in bigger than the part that goes through (a 1/2 in shower arm is 7/8 in across, so 1 1/8 in; a single-handle valve cartridge is about 2 in, so 2 1/4 in), and small enough that the flange or plate overlaps it by at least 1/2 in all round. (4) Cut with a hole saw, slowly and with the plank face up on the foam board, so the PVC does not melt or crack; never a twist drill. (5) Seal the flutes that the hole opens with a dab of sealant.
  13. Glue the planks on, starting at the nook. (1) Dab the flexible adhesive on the plywood in beads about 8 in apart down each plank's width, never near a joint or an edge, and only as many planks as you can fit in the open time on the cartridge (cool or warm, as it says). (2) Set plank 2 first, its rear edge exactly on the line at 9 3/8, resting on 1/4 in spacers on the pan, plumb with a level; press it flat from the middle outward and roll the face. (3) Slide plank 3 in (tongue into groove, closing the joint to a hairline, not forcing it), then plank 4 the same way. (4) Put plank 1 in last, its tongue into plank 2's groove, 1/8 in off the rear end. (5) Hold the planks with strips of masking tape for the first hour, keep the van within the temperatures the cartridge allows while it cures, and wipe any squeeze-out at once. (6) Leave the spacers in until the adhesive has set.
  14. Line the nook. Glue the two cut-outs to the plywood back of the nook as its back (tongue into groove), then its floor, its ceiling and its two sides from the offcuts (the floor and the ceiling are 17 1/4 x 4 1/2 in, the sides 16 x 4 1/2 in), each in dabs of adhesive and each butted to the back, with a bead of sealant in every inside corner. The front edges are left open, with no lip: a bungee cord across the front keeps things in. Use a 4 in roll: the nook is about 4 1/4 in clear inside the lining.
  15. Seal and trim. Fit the J-shaped end trim over the plank edges at the rear end and at the cab end, glued to the plywood, with a bead of sealant in the 1/8 in gap behind each (drawing F); fit the top trim strip under the ceiling; fill the 1/4 in gap at the pan with flexible sealant and tool it, pressing it into the open flutes; seal every nook corner and behind the head and valve flanges; wipe the excess off straight away. Let it cure a day.
  16. Seal around the valve and the head (drawing I), after the planks are glued and the valve and the shower arm are in, with the trim plates off. (1) Wipe the plank around each hole and the back of each plate with isopropyl alcohol and let it dry. (2) Dry-fit the plate: it must cover its hole by at least 1/2 in all round. (3) Run a continuous 1/8 in bead of neutral-cure silicone or MS polymer on the back of the plate, in a closed ring about 1/4 in in from its edge, with no gaps (overlap the ends). (4) Press the plate on and tighten its own screws, which go into the valve fitting and never into the plank alone, until the bead squeezes out evenly all round; do not over-tighten, which cracks the plank or the plate. (5) Stick painter's tape on the panel 1/4 in outside the plate's edge, run a thin bead in the gap between the tape and the plate, smooth it in one pass with a wet finger or a tool, and pull the tape off before it skins. (6) Leave the cartridge and the handle alone: the valve's own seals look after them. (7) Wait a day, then run the shower for ten minutes and look for wet behind (from under the floor or through the valve access); touch up any pinhole. Check these beads once a year and redo any that crack or go black.
  17. Finish and test. Fit the head and valve, hang the curtains from the ceiling rail and the nook cord (the Wet bath page), then run the shower for ten minutes with the curtains closed and check under the floor and along the bottom of the back wall, and where the cab-end curtain hangs by the fridge, for any wet.
